Trigger Warning: What is discussed in this paper is the two primary views on what happened to Dinah in the story of Shechem and Dinah in Genesis 34: was she raped or did she consenting to having relations with Shechem?

What Happened in Genesis 34? Was Dinah Raped or Was She Consenting?

Joshua M. Haub

March 8, 2026

p1What Happened in Genesis 34?

What exactly happened to Dinah in Genesis 34 has been the topic of much debate among scholars. Was she the victim of rape or a consenting party in a pre-marital sexual encounter? And what does one make of the reactions of the Jacobites and Canaanites to the incident? It seems to me, based on my research, that the more plausible answer is that she was raped by Shechem, which resulted in the sons of Jacob retaliating against the Canaanites.

Bechtel’s “What if Dinah is Not Raped?”

L. Bechtel, in her article, argues that Dinah was not raped but, instead, was a consenting party.1 Early in her article, she deals with the debated verb ‘nh (Genesis 34:2-3). She notes that the verb is found in its Piel form and that “one of its meanings is ‘to humiliate intensely.’”2 This idea of humiliation and/or shaming in this event is central to Bechtel’s article. She goes on to discuss the ancient Israelite view of shame and humiliation in the context of a group-oriented society (which uses shame and humiliation to keep its members “in line” for the sake of preservation). Bechtel argues “that within a sexual context the verb ‘nh in the Piel indicates the ‘humiliation’ or ‘shaming’ of a woman through certain kinds of sexual intercourse including rape, though not necessarily.”3 In Bechtel’s research, ancient Israel viewed sexual intercourse as shameful when it (1) violates pre-established “marital, family or community bonding and obligation” or (2) the sexual intercourse is done when there is no future marriage or family bonding in view.4

p2Bechtel turns to a key passage (split into three parts): Deuteronomy 22:23-9. The first part, Deuteronomy 22:23-4, Bechtel interprets as an encounter where there is no rape. Instead, the shame caused by this encounter is because of its violation of social bonding. The verb ‘nh is used in verse 24. The next part Bechtel discusses is Deuteronomy 22:28-9. She notes that again there is no rape and “the verb ‘nh is used” in verse 29.5 This encounter is shameful because “there is no request for future bonding, and therefore, he has humiliated (‘nh, v. 29) her.”6 The final part is Deuteronomy 23:25-7, which Bechtel does believe is an account of rape. Interestingly, she notes, “there is rape, but no shame, the verb ‘nh is not used.”7

Now Bechtel turns to the story of Genesis 34. She argues that from the use of the verb in Deuteronomy 22, and the emotions and actions of Shechem and his people, that this story is not about rape. She notes, “sociological studies reveal that rapists feel hostility and hatred toward their victims, not love.”8 Also, Shechem does not apology for the encounter. Instead, the text, according to Bechtel, seems to regard Shechem and his people as honorable. Bechtel states, “All of this diminishes the likelihood that rape was seen to have occurred.”9

Shemesh’s “Rape is Rape is Rape”

Y. Shemesh (contra Betchel) believes the Dinah incident was in fact, rape.10 She begins by addressing the narrative of Genesis 34. In this narrative, Shemesh explains why the narrator does not explain Dinah’s “side of the story” (but does give the reader Shechem’s). According to p3Shemesh, this is because the story is concerned with the Israelite versus Canaanite relation.11 It is the family (nation) of Israel who is wronged by the Canaanite family (nation). This sets the stage for how this atrocity is to be viewed by the reader.

There are two leading theories as to how this story ought to be interpreted, which Shemesh calls “the Seduction Theory” and “the Abduction Marriage Theory,” respectively.12 For our purposes we will look at the first theory. According to this theory, because the Bible does not explicitly say that Dinah was raped, she was instead seduced by Shechem. In this view, the verb ‘innah is believed to be a reference to one of three things: (1) the loss of her virginity, (2) “forbidden (though consensual) relations…[as found in] Deut 22,24 and… [possibly] Ez 22,10f,” or (3) “merely… sexual intercourse.”13

However, Shemesh finds this theory inadequate, especially in light of the verbs used in the passage: “seized … lay … abused.”14 In Shemesh’s perspective, Shechem is forcefully engaging in sexual relations with Dinah (seemingly without her consent), which is, by modern definitions, rape. This view, according to Shemesh, is backed up by the following verse in which Shechem speaks “tenderly to her.”15 A rapist may engage in such speech in order “to placate their victims."16

Shemesh goes on to deal with the verb ‘innah.17 First, Shemesh states that “nowhere does the Bible use the verb ‘innah to describe normal and legitimate relations between a man and his p4wife."18 Next, she states that when the verb is used “in the pi’el in other than a sexual context the connotation is always negative, involving pressure and distress…the verb is used for the application of physical or psychological violence.”19 The only difficulty with this view is Deuteronomy 22:23-4, which Shemesh explains is a law that punishes girls who do not try and stop the encounter (by crying out).

Shemesh moves into explaining the Seduction Theory, which emphasizes Shechem’s love contra a rapist’s supposed lust. Shemesh, however, believes that Shechem’s actions after the encounter (speaking “tenderly to her” and offering to pay a dowry) may be in line with psychological research concerning the mind of a rapist.20 Shemesh concludes the section by addressing “three categories of rape.”21 These are anger rape, power rape, and sadistic rape. In Shemesh’s research, she concluded that the most likely answer is that “Shechem seems to suit the second and most prevalent category, power rape. The phenomenon of the rapist who sees himself as a lover is found only in this category.”22 Therefore, Shemesh concludes that this is probably an account of rape.

Analysis

Answering the Dinah question is very difficult, and both sides add valuable information to the discussion. To synthesize the data from both Betchel and Shemesh, the fundamental question seems to be impossible to answer: “What were Dinah’s intentions?” The text never says. So, instead, what does the text say? There was a man (Shechem) who “saw… took…lay…”23 What neither article discusses, which I believe is a key point, is the usage of “saw and took” in p5Genesis. This theme can be seen in, for example, Gen 3:6 (“saw…took”) and 13:10 (“saw…chose”).24 Both times the narrator of Genesis is condemning the actions of the subject. Now, in Genesis 34, the narrator uses the same concept for Shechem. Shechem is following the pattern of “bad decision-making” found in the book.

Bechtel is helpful when discussing Deuteronomy 22:23-9. There are very strict laws in ancient Israel concerning rape and consenting incidents. If the author of Genesis is writing Genesis 34 with the laws of Deuteronomy in mind, then Bechtel’s emphasis on this passage in the discussion of Genesis 34 may lean in favor of her conclusion that Dinah was not raped.25 However, it is difficult to know whether the author is narrating Genesis 34 based on these laws.

When it comes to the psychology of the situation, Shemesh seems to be correct in her evaluation of the perpetrator and his victim. Shechem may genuinely love Dinah, but he has taken advantage of her. The pairing of 34:2 and 7 seems to imply a sexual encounter that is more than simply pre-marital sex. The text implies what has happened behind the scenes without outright exposing the shameful event.

Conclusion

This sexual encounter between Shechem and Dinah, based on my research, seems more plausibly an account of rape rather than seduction. What happened to Dinah was a tragedy. The discussion of what happened to Dinah is not over. There are still more questions to be answered. There are many things that we do not know, and probably will never know about what happened p6in Genesis 34. However, what we do know is that God, despite this event, worked His perfect plan through imperfect people. In the end, the blessing of Jacob (in Genesis 49) passed by Simeon and Levi to Judah, through whom Jesus came, which was God’s plan from the beginning.
