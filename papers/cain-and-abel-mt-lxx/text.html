p1Cain and Abel: The MT and LXX Versions1

The Genesis 4 account of Cain and Abel tells us surprisingly little about what led up to the event and why Cain decides to murder his brother. Even more surprising is the NT authors’ interpretation of the event. While the MT leaves the event vague, the NT interprets it through the lens of “righteous Abel” and “wicked Cain.”2 Not only is he condemned for murdering his brother, but he appears also to be condemned for his unapproved sacrifice. It seems that the reason for the discrepancies in the text has to do with the LXX’s understanding of the story. It is this interpretation which leads to the NT authors’ view of Cain. In this paper, I examine some of the key discrepancies between these texts and show how these have led to the NT’s understanding of the two brothers.

The Masoretic Text

The MT leaves the reader with many questions, beginning with Cain’s birth. What does the end of Genesis 4:1 mean? The LXX and Vulgate have “through God” while Targum Onkelos has “from before God” and the Peshitta has “to God.”3 Pinker’s suggestion that our MT has been incorrectly preserved as אֶת־יְהוָה instead of תֵת־יְהוָה is intriguing.4 If he is correct, the proper reading of the text would be “I have acquired a man to give God.”5 According to this reading, p2Eve desired to dedicate her firstborn son to God. This explains why the Genesis author records the meaning of Cain’s name but not Abel’s: Cain fails to live up to his calling.6

The next question begins in v. 3. Why are Cain and Abel bringing offerings to the Lord? This is easy to overlook because offerings were required under the Law of Moses. However, this story predates the Law. The author has left out key details and thus builds the story on ambiguity. This leaves the reader confused when God accepts Abel’s offering but rejects Cain’s. The MT gives no explanation.7 Some have said that vv. 3-4 imply that Cain’s “an offering” was improper compared to Abel’s “firstlings of his flock and of their fat portions.”8 However, this is simply an attempt to understand the ambiguous situation.9 Lohr helpfully notes that while some details could point to Abel’s offering being better, others could likewise point in the other direction.10 While Lohr may go too far here, his reservations concerning vv. 3-4 are well taken.

Based on the story we have in the MT, Cain is understandably angry. God has seemingly rejected him without reason.11 God, then, speaks with Cain and questions his reaction to the p3rejection. The hardest verse in this narrative is 4:7.12 Westermann is probably correct in saying that our MT is corrupted here.13 Wenham argues that v. 7 is either about God forgiving or accepting him.14 At the very least, God warns Cain of the future. The statement: “Sin is at the door” (4:7) may imply that Cain has not sinned yet, but he is on the verge if he does not regain his composure.

Cain leaves the conversation with God and decides what to do next. The MT seems incomplete in v. 8: “Cain told Abel his brother. And it came about when they were in the field, that Cain rose up against Abel his brother and killed him.” What did Cain tell his brother? One alternate reading is, “Cain was looking for Abel his brother.”15 Another, probably better, reading is what the LXX records, see discussion below. The placement of “in the field” may be evidence of premeditation as seen in the Law (cf. Deut. 22:25-27).16 The Lord returns to confront Cain, who lies, and punishes him by banishment. However, because of Cain’s pleading, God gives him a sign to prevent others from harming him.17 The narrative of Cain and Abel ends in 4:16-17 with Cain leaving God’s presence and heading eastward into “the land of Nod” (4:16), which is probably a literary way of saying “land of wandering.”18

The MT leaves the reader with few explanations for his or her questions. Why did the brothers offer sacrifices? Why did God accept Abel’s and reject Cain’s? Why did Cain decide to murder his brother? It may be tempting to turn to the NT for answers, but the NT’s p4understanding of the narrative is based on the LXX, not MT. Considering the MT alone, Cain’s grave sin is not found in his offering but in the murder of his brother. Why did the author arrange the story this way? Wenham writes that Adam begins the Genesis cycle of God choosing the younger son over the older.19 This cycle can be seen in some of Genesis’ main characters, such as Abraham (Isaac chosen over Ishmael) and Isaac (Jacob chosen over Esau). The closest the MT gets to offering a reason for Cain’s rejected sacrifice is God’s counsel in v. 7, unless one accepts the usual interpretation of vv. 3-4.20 Perhaps the reason there is no explicit statement as to why Cain was rejected is because God’s decision was arbitrary; there was no sin involved.21 He simply chose Abel over Cain (similar to when God chose Jacob over Esau, see Romans 9:10-13). However, while Cain’s offering was not accepted, God still desired for Cain to “do well” (4:7) and so comforts him. Cain pushes God away and falls into sin. It is only now, after the murder, that God punishes him.

The Septuagint

The LXX text seems more like an interpretive reading rather than a strict translation.22 These differences begin with 4:1 with the LXX translating the end of the verse as “I have acquired a p5man through God.”23 But the first difference that is striking in its portrayal of Cain and Abel is v. 2 where the LXX uses “δέ” to separate Cain and Abel’s professions.24 The LXX has already begun pitting the brothers against each other.

Then, the offerings of both brothers is designated differently. Cain offers a “θυσία (‘sacrifice’) (v. 5)” while Abel offers a “δῶρον (‘gift’) (v. 4).”25 Lohr comments that “both are a מנחה (‘gift’ or ‘offering’) in the Hebrew.”26 In Genesis, this Hebrew term is consistently translated as “δῶρον” (cf. LXX Genesis 32:14, 19, 21, 22; 33:10; 43:11, 15, 25, 26).27 Lohr cites Philo’s explanation that these terms, while similar, invoke different images: the “sacrifice” is essentially a partial offering while the “gift” is a complete one.28 Philo states:

What difference is there between a gift and a sacrifice [in Gen. 4:4-5]? He who slaughters a sacrifice [θυσίαν], after dividing it, pours the blood on the altar and takes the flesh home. But he who offers something as a gift [δῶρον] offers the whole of it, it seems, to him who receives it. And the lover of self is a divider, as was Cain, while the lover of God is a giver, as was Abel.29

Lohr’s conclusion is that “the translator wished to distinguish the quality of the brothers’ offerings.”30 Therefore, the LXX’s translation has led readers, like Philo, to exalt Abel and diminish Cain.

p6Likewise, the Greek translation of God’s viewing of the brothers’ sacrifices is different. The LXX says that God “ἐπεῖδεν” (“looked upon”) Abel’s offering but did not “προσέσχεν” (“pay attention to”) to Cain’s.31 Lohr argues, based on the rest of the LXX’s usage of these terms, that ἐφοράω is used for God looking favorable upon someone or something while προσέχω, in Genesis and Deuteronomy, seems to be the idea of “keeping closely” or “clinging to.” So in this verse, God did not “προσέσχεν” Cain “is the idea of rejecting or forsaking.”32 Thus the LXX continues interpreting the event, causing the readers to follow suit.33

When we arrive at 4:7, the differences between versions become even more apparent. The text says, “If you offer correctly but do not divide correctly, have you not sinned? Be still; his recourse is to you, and you will rule over him.” According to the LXX, God tells Cain that his offering (“fruit of the earth” [4:3]) was good. What he did wrong was not “divid[ing] correctly.” What does this mean? Philo thinks it means he did not give God his best, choosing, instead, to keep it for himself.34 This is absent in the MT which says God told Cain that if he does well he will be “lifted up.” The LXX, therefore, seeks to make clearer the more cryptic MT, unless an argument for the LXX following an earlier (and less corrupted) MT can be made.

Second, 4:7 (LXX) ends with “Be still; his recourse is to you, and you will rule over him.” What does this mean? In the MT, the referent is “sin”, but the LXX leaves it ambiguous (“p7his”/“him” or “its”/“it”).35 The LXX leaves out the statement that sin is at the door and Cain must master it. Instead, the LXX has, “rule over him.” Lohr takes this to mean “Abel’s turning…will be toward Cain and Cain will rule him; Cain, however, is to remain still, and not commit further sin.”36

Next, the LXX records the statement from Cain in 4:8 that the MT leaves out: “And Kain said to his brother Habel, ‘Let us go through into the plain.’”37 This statement, which I think is probably better than the unfortunate MT’s blank, further illustrates Cain’s wickedness—not only is he angry but his decision to murder his brother is premeditated.38 He takes Abel to the perfect spot (into the plain) and there kills him. If 4:8a is deemed to be absent from the original Vorlage, it probably is an attempt by the LXX to either fix the text or continue its interpretive view of Cain.

The narrative continues on until 4:13 where the LXX has, “And Kain said to the Lord, ‘My guilt is too great for me to be forgiven!’” This is different than the MT which has “My punishment is too great to bear!”39 The LXX has added “to be forgiven.” This gives the story a moral element.40 However, Philo seems to read “to be forgiven” (τοῦ ἀφεθῆναί με) not as “forgiveness” but as separation from God, which is another way ἀφεθῆναί can be understood.41 Unless he is right, the MT’s reading makes Cain’s statement more about his distress concerning p8the punishment while the LXX’s reading seems to be more about his inability to receive forgiveness, and therefore continues the LXX’s theme of emphasizing Cain’s sinfulness and wickedness. He is, then, banished from the presence of the Lord and given “a sign” (4:15) to prevent others from killing him.

Analysis

What should one make of the differences between the MT and LXX versions of the story? While it may be tempting to assert that the NT is unreliable, it is better to view its authors as simply being faithful to the translation used in the first century (and the social memory of the story), much like modern readers with the MT.42 The NT authors relay an understanding of the narrative based on their version of it, using it for their purposes.43

The point of the MT version is about man’s continued journey “eastward” beginning in Gen. 3. Adam and Eve sin by eating of the fruit, and, when confronted, blame others for their sin, which results in God sending mankind out and setting up cherubim “at the east of the garden” (3:24). The next sin is Cain’s murder of Abel, in which God asks essentially the same question from ch. 3 (4:10; cf. 3:13) and uses similar concepts (e.g., 4:7; cf. 3:16).44 Cain has escalated the sin-problem, taking it further than his parents. While Adam and Eve disobeyed by eating forbidden fruit, they resort to blame rather than lie. However, Cain murders and lies. Adam and p9Eve are embarrassed, Cain is not. So God must send man farther east, away from his presence. Therefore, the MT’s inclusion of 4:1-16 appears to do two things: (1) advance the effects of sin and (2) explain the birth of Seth (the one whose lineage extends mankind through the flood) in 4:25-26. And so the Genesis author leaves out details which are irrelevant to his purposes; he is not interested in giving a detailed account of the story and therefore is not interested in answering the reader’s questions.45

The point of the LXX version is essentially the same, but as argued above, adds the element of Cain’s improper worship. Cain is not just a murderer and liar, but one who incorrectly worships God. The NT grows attached to this element (cf. Heb. 11:4) and uses it to describe present-day issues between true Christians (Abelites) and false worshippers (Cainites).46 It is therefore not hard to see why the NT would use this story.47

Conclusion

I have examined some of the key differences between the MT and LXX versions of Gen. 4:1-16 and briefly commented on the NT’s usage of the LXX. The LXX, while being a more interpretive telling of the story, provides a helpful model for understanding early readings of the story and how the NT adapts it for issues in the first century. This paper illustrates the reminder for modern readers to read the OT and NT within their own contexts without imposing one upon the other. One can appreciate both while also acknowledging their differences. Allowing the MT to speak for itself, the author’s intent can be better understood and thus one’s interpretation of the narrative can align closer with it as he or she works through the Genesis narrative.
